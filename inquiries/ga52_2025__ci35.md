# Supplemental II.2 — Whether additional nominations from the floor (RAO 3-4) would be allowed if the AC did not bring a Stated Clerk nominee

*The CCB ruled that floor nominations are permitted under RAO 3-4 even if the Administrative Committee does not bring a Stated Clerk nominee.*

## Verbatim record

Scriptures, and things circumstantial. In things necessary to life, they must be derived only from express statements from the Word of God, or by good and necessary consequence deduced from Scripture. In things circumstantial, in worship and government—in matters common to human actions and societies—they are to be ordered by the light of nature, and Christian prudence, according to the general rules of the Word. That is to say, the terms "necessary” and “circumstantial” as they have been historically employed in questions about worship are as follows. Something necessary is something required by Scripture. Something circumstantial is something not addressed by Scripture, but is necessary because of the circumstances. Concerning things necessary, the government of the church has no discretion, it cannot add, nor take away. Concerning things circumstantial, the church has discretion and is called to act with prudence. And, concerning circumstantials, the Lord of the conscience has left it free from the doctrines and commandments of men. Thus, a simple question: is the matter of a benediction in a worship service a thing necessary, or a thing circumstantial? If it is a circumstantial, it is liable to the government's discretion, and also, it cannot be imposed as a conscientious obligation (certainly not on the grounds of a vow to be in submission to the brethren, as that vow can only apply to one's prudential judgments, not one's conscientious convictions.). If it is a necessity, it must be shown to be so from Scripture (by express or implied warrant), and it is a moral obligation for all. In this case, it could not matter more what arguments the Session believed were in favor of its decision. The arguments employed essentially betray the ground upon which the action is justified. If the argument is that the Scripture requires the action, it is no longer a circumstantial, it is a Scriptural necessity. If the argument is that merely the circumstances make necessary the action, then it is circumstantial. In this case, the burden of proof is on the proposer, a fundamental principle of logic. The Session had proposed a rule; the burden was on the Session to vindicate its action as either a Scriptural necessity or a circumstantial necessity. The Session mixed the two, and succeeded in neither.

The historic Reformed doctrine is not that the Session must deduce from Scripture the way to end a service. If you could deduce that from Scripture it is a thing necessary according Confession of Faith 1.6. It is things that are neither expressly set down, nor are implied by Scripture, that constitute circumstancials.

Further, if the close of the worship service is a part of the ministry of the Word, the minister decides as to his service. If the close has only to do with the circumstance that there must be some sort of closing, the Session may decide, but it may not bind the minister in the matter.

The General Assembly, in its responsibility to oversee the Constitution of the church, and itself having inherently all the powers of any counsel of Elders, has never proposed, and the Directory for Worship does not include, benedictions as a Scriptural element of worship.

If this true for the church, as such, it must be true for any particular expression
of the church, such as the Session. Furthermore, with respect to the Session
and the Presbytery, their judgments in each case, according to its kind, are
subject to the review and correction by the court next higher. That is to say,
the judgment as to whether a matter is necessary or circumstantial, as well as
the judgment of the matter materially within whatever framework is adopted,
is properly subject to review and correction.

In this case, complainants from the Session, from their argumentation, clearly do not believe the benediction is a circumstantial, but in their Complaint, and according to the Commission's decision, they would have the Presbytery, and the Assembly, treat it as such. On the other hand, if they believe, as they must, according to their argumentation, that the benediction is a thing necessary, they have clearly gone beyond the Directory for Worship, and the Scripture, and furthermore, have failed to meet the burden of proof. According to Complainants, from the point of view of the BCO, either the listing of elements of worship is in error, or the benediction must be, at best, a circumstantial. To require an element beyond this, as necessary, denies this stance, and illicitly adds to the Directory, and thus is forbidden. Recall BCO 12-5.e., “To exercise, in accordance with the Directory for Worship...."

With respect to this point, the Respondent's arguments set forth in their Brief are, in my judgment, dispositive.[^fn-ga52-p901-n21] Here are displayed the arguments that should have informed the Commission's decision.

### Regarding the allegation that PP infringed upon the rights and duties of VPC Session:

As we see it, the question hinges upon three issues: 1) is REs Martinez and Cook's definition of benediction correct? 2) is it Scriptural or Constitutional to require that worship must be concluded with a "formal benediction” as defined by REs Martinez and Cook? and 3) Does the ruling of VPC session go beyond the authority granted a session by the BCO, potentially endangering the freedom of a TE's conscience?

<u>Firstly</u>, in their March 23 complaint to PP, REs Martinez and Cook defined a benediction as “...not prayers, or praises to God, but pronouncements of God's blessings upon his covenant people."[^fn-ga52-p901-n22] They go on to distinguish benediction especially from doxologies based on the "movement" of the two. "[A] key difference between a doxology and a benediction is that doxologies are praises directed to God from God's people and benedictions are the blessing of God directed to God's people from God himself,"[^fn-ga52-p901-n23] eventually concluding that “doxologies are not benedictions." [^fn-ga52-p901-n24] However, is this rigid definition of benediction warranted?

The English word “benediction" is derived from the Latin language. In Latin, we find the roots bene, which means “"well, rightly, kindly, prosperously," [^fn-ga52-p901-n25] and dictio, “a setting forth in words, stating, declaring."[^fn-ga52-p901-n26] Together, benedictus means “a good word," without any apparent regard to whom the word is spoken to or by.
